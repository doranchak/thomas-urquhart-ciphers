The Jewel (Ekskybalauron), 1652

Sir Thomas Urquhart, ΕΚΣΚΥΒΑΛΑΥΡΟΝ: or, The Discovery of a most exquisite Jewel… found in the kennel of Worcester-streets, the day after the Fight (London: printed by James Cottrel, sold by Richard Baddeley, 1652). Octavo, 284 numbered pages plus front matter. Page references are to the 1652 edition, from the EEBO-TCP transcription (A95749).

What is it?
A prisoner's plea for freedom, wrapped in a defence of Scotland's honour.

Urquhart wrote it in London in early 1652 as a prisoner of war on parole after the royalist defeat at Worcester (3 September 1651). It is addressed to the English Parliament and Council of State. Its title page announces a "Vindication of the honour of Scotland" from the infamy brought on it by "the Rigid Presbyterian party".

What is it for?
To win back his liberty and his estates at Cromarty.

The first sentence of the preface says so: the book's "scope" is to entreat Parliament "to grant to Sir Thomas Urquhart of Cromarty his former liberty, and the enjoyment of his own inheritance". Everything else serves that request: the promised Universal Language, the parade of brilliant Scots, the attack on the Kirk, and the case for union with England.

Why is it remembered?
For "the Admirable Crichton".

A quarter of the book retells the life and death of James Crichton, the Scottish prodigy killed at Mantua in 1582. Urquhart's version is the main source of the "Admirable Crichton" legend. The book also contains the first announcement of his Universal Language, a fantastical family tree back to Adam, and, in some copies, the "Cyfral Octastich" cipher.

Overall summary

The book pretends to be something found by chance. A frame story tells how Urquhart's manuscripts, more than three thousand sheets of them, were plundered at Worcester and used by soldiers to wrap raisins and light their pipes. One bundle was rescued from the mud beside a heap of 27 dead men. It turns out to be part of the Preface to his Universal Language, and this fragment is the "jewel" of the title. The narrator, who writes about "Sir Thomas Urquhart" in the third person and signs himself Christianus Presbyteromastix ("a Christian scourge of Presbyterians"), prints the fragment. He then argues that a man capable of such a work deserves his freedom and his lands.

From there the book widens into a sweeping defence of the Scottish nation. Since Scotland was being called covetous and treacherous, Urquhart sets out to prove that the fault lies with a few, chiefly the Presbyterian ministers, and not with the nation. His proof is a vast catalogue of Scots who won glory abroad as soldiers, duellists and scholars, crowned by the romance of the Admirable Crichton. He blames the Kirk's greed and its claim to "spiritual sovereignty" for Scotland's disgrace. He then urges the full incorporation of Scotland with England, quoting at length a 1607 Commons speech by Sir Francis Bacon. He closes with a rhetorical analysis of his own book and a formal list of demands. By his own account the whole thing was written and printed in fourteen working days, with the typesetter often setting his sheets before the ink was dry.

Section by section

Front matter (unnumbered pages)
Title page

The Greek title Ekskybalauron is Urquhart's coinage. It roughly means "gold out of dung", a jewel picked out of the gutter, fitting the story of papers rescued from Worcester's streets. The page also carries a riddling couplet in which grammatical case abbreviations stand in for words ("O voc. thou'rt a nom. Book…"), and the maxim Efficiens & finis sunt sibi invicem causae ("the maker and the purpose are causes of each other").

The Epistle Liminary

A frank statement of purpose. It also tells the story of the book's making:

  • The book entreats Parliament and the Council of State to restore Urquhart's liberty and inheritance. His house has an English garrison in it, and he has "not received the value of one farthing" from his lands in the half-year since his capture.
  • It speaks for Scotland and argues for "incorporating of both nations into one". It promises a full exposé of "Presbyterian plots" another time.
  • Urquhart planned only five sheets. Then he read two newsbooks: one reported the Presbytery of Aberdeen's proceedings against Sir Alexander Irvine of Drum, the other a petition of the Scottish commons against their ministers. Hearing the English talk of "the perfidious Scot… the covetous Scot", he decided to add the "martial and literatory endowments" of famous Scots.
  • He describes racing the compositor: he would tear off "parcels of ten or twelve lines" for the typesetter, finishing the whole book "in the space of fourteen working-daies… from the first notion of the brain, till the last motion of the press".
  • He explains the title and the third-person narration, and signs as Christianus Presbyteromastix.
The genealogies

Two numbered lists. The first gives 153 chiefs of the name of Urquhart, from "1 Adam", Seth, Enos and Noah, through dozens of invented Greek-named ancestors (Esormon, Astioremon, Thaleros…), down to "152 Sir Thomas" (his father) and "153 Sir Thomas" (himself). The second gives 146 of their mothers. It is a compressed version of his Pantochronochanon (Promptuary of Time), and pure Urquhart: grandiose, learned-sounding, and largely fantasy. A list of errata follows.

Part one: the found papers and the Universal Language
pp. 1–7 · The discovery at Worcester (frame story)

It opens with a verse motto ("He should obtain all his desires, Who offers more then he requires") and a vivid account of the sack of Worcester. Soldiers break into Master Spilsbury's house and find seven portmanteaus of manuscripts, "sixscore and eight quires and a half". They first throw them on the floor, then carry them off for wrapping groceries, lighting tobacco "and posteriour uses". One quire and two loose sheets, stuck to the ground by rain beside "a heap of seven and twenty dead men", are picked up by a servant of a Master Braughton. They prove to be part of the Preface to a Universal Language, whose full manuscript (Preface, Grammar and Lexicon) was lost along with treatises on metaphysics, mathematics, morals and more. Asked whether he could rewrite it, Urquhart answers that he could, "if he wanted not encouragement", but not without the freedom of his own estate.

pp. 7–43 · The Introduction to the Universal Language

The "found" Preface, in numbered articles:

  • A theory of language. Words are signs of things and should mirror the order of things, so a perfect language would let a hearer know a word's meaning from its sound.
  • A survey of the defects of existing languages: inadequate alphabets, borrowing between tongues, and the poverty of the learned languages.
  • A defence of new inventions against those who say "there is nothing new under the sun".
  • A list of the language's advantages: "sixty and three" in this edition. Among them are eleven genders, ten cases, four numbers, words that read backwards and forwards, numbers expressed in single words, and the claim that a ten-year-old could learn it in three months.

It ends by teasing "the generous reader" with the grammar and lexicon "which I am to evulge", and they never appeared. This section was reused, expanded to 66 advantages, as Book 1 of Logopandecteision (1653).

pp. 43–55 · Axioms of merit and the "secret in learning"

A second set of 22 numbered axioms on the value of learning and the duty to reward discoverers ("Who discovereth most of the best good, deserveth the best recompence"). From them Urquhart "deduces" that he should have his inheritance back. In return he offers a secret method by which students would learn in three years what now takes five, which he values at over £10,000 a year to the nation. He stakes his whole estate that no one else can show any insight into it within half a year.

Part two: the vindication of Scotland
pp. 55–64 · No nation is wholly bad

Being a Scot is no fault, Urquhart argues, since no one chooses where he is born. Every nation has its proverbial vice (proud Spaniards, inconstant French, quarrelsome Poles) and its exceptions. Scotland's present reputation for "covetousness and hypocrisie" comes from ministers who taught the people to equate holiness with obedience to their injunctions and filled their own purses. He contrasts this with the great reputation Scots once had abroad.

pp. 64–81 · Scottish soldiers abroad

A roll-call of Scots who served foreign princes. In Swedish service under Gustavus Adolphus there were Alexander Leslie, Patrick Ruthven, James King, the Ramsays and Hepburns; in the Emperor's service, Colonel Leslie (made a hereditary Marquis of the Empire) and Colonel Gordon (High Chamberlain); in the Dutch service against Spain, the Balfours, Colonel Edmond, Brog and others. Interleaved are tall tales of the "ever-renowned Earl of Bothwell" in Spanish service, who killed a lion with a single sword and routed bands of hired assassins across Europe. A detour on John Napier reports the rumour of his secret engine able to clear four miles of ground of every living creature. Napier refused to reveal it, saying there were already "too many devices" for the ruin of man.

pp. 81–148 · The Admirable Crichton (the centrepiece)

The book's longest and most famous episode, told as a chivalric romance of James Crichton (1560–1582):

  • The duel at Mantua (pp. 82–92). An Italian swordsman has killed three challengers at the Duke's court. Crichton defeats him and gives the 1,500 pistoles won to the three widows.
  • The disputation at Paris (pp. 93–103). Crichton challenges the University to dispute with him on any science or art, "in any of these twelve Languages" from Hebrew to Slavonic, in verse or prose, and astonishes all the professors.
  • The comedy (pp. 103–116). Back in Mantua he acts a five-hour comedy of his own making, playing fifteen characters himself, to expose the vices of the court.
  • The love affair (pp. 117–136). A long, lush and erotic account of his night with a noble lady of the court, the woman the Prince himself loved best.
  • The death (pp. 136–148). Leaving her house, he is set upon by ten masked men and kills six of them. He is about to disarm the leader when a courtier cries "Hold, hold, kill not the Prince!" Crichton kneels and offers the Prince the hilt of his own sword, and the drunken, jealous Prince runs him through the heart with it. The Prince, half-mad with remorse, attempts suicide; the lady grieves for the rest of her life.
"The verity of this story I have here related concerning this incomparable Crichtoun, may be certified by above two thousand men yet living, who have known him."p. 148. In fact Crichton had been dead for seventy years.

Crichton was real, a precocious scholar killed in Mantua in 1582 by Vincenzo Gonzaga. Most of Urquhart's detail is embellishment or invention, but his version fixed the legend.

pp. 148–162 · More Scots in foreign service

The catalogue resumes. In Venice there is Captain William Scot, vice-admiral and "terror of Mahometan Navigators". Others served Poland, Muscovy and the "Scyths and Sarmats"; one Scot was even elected king of "Bucharia". A long anecdote follows the champion Sinclair: a fencing match before the Emperor at Vienna, and the killing of seven Spaniards "one after another" in a single afternoon. Urquhart then explains why he names almost no Scots who fought in the recent British wars: most were in thrall to the Kirk.

pp. 162–171 · The Kirk and the Covenanting levies

A bitter account of how Presbyterian committees ran the Scottish war effort. They rejected good men and horses as lacking zeal for the Covenant and took money instead. In Urquhart's phrase, they stayed "at home to hatch young chickens of pecunial interest". Officers who obeyed them should be remembered only as warning beacons.

pp. 171–228 · Scottish men of learning

A parallel catalogue of scholars and writers:

  • Alexander Ross and his many books.
  • Buchanan and Arthur Johnston, for their Psalm paraphrases.
  • John Barclay, author of Argenis.
  • Scottish professors in France, Spain and Italy, and Catholic and Protestant divines.
  • Gordon of Straloch the geographer, Duncan Liddel the mathematician, and Napier again.

There are digressions on climate, national temperament and the origin of laws (pp. 187–194). There is a satirical passage on Sir William Alexander, Earl of Stirling, whose courtier ambitions spoiled his poetry, and on his scheme selling Nova Scotia baronetcies (pp. 207–212). There are notes on manuscripts lost or bought up by Archbishop Laud (pp. 215–216). Urquhart appeals directly to two Scots with influence in Parliament to support his petition (p. 222), and closes with a swordsman praised by the Earl of Newcastle and the late Earl of Essex.

pp. 228–252 · The case against the Kirk

Urquhart's sharpest polemic. Presbyterian government cannot coexist with monarchy, as the histories of Geneva, Bohemia, the French wars of religion and Mary Queen of Scots show. In Scotland it left the young King Charles with no more real power over the "Presbyterian Senate" than a "mock-king", and it turned against Montrose. The Kirk hoards wealth while despising learning. He names his own ministers at Cromarty, Kirkmichael and Cullicudden (Gilbert Anderson, Robert Williamson, Charles Pape), whose greed and quarrels, down to a dispute over a church pew, have harmed him. He sketches an alternative: learned, gentle ministers who would be loved and enriched by their parishes, especially if Scotland were joined to England.

Part three: the union, and the closing plea
pp. 252–274 · For union with England

A union "not heterogeneal… bound by the frost of a conquering Sword; but homogeneated by naturalization" would heal Scotland. To make the case, Urquhart quotes at length the speech Sir Francis Bacon gave in the House of Commons "in the fifth year of King James his raign" (1607), urging the House to naturalize the Scots, "Master Speaker" and all. He follows it with Sir Edward Coke's reasoning from Calvin's Case. In his own voice he then argues for incorporating England, Scotland and Wales into one Parliament at Westminster, so as to "raise the Isle of Britain to that height of glory, that it will become formidable to all the world". He apologises for "insisting so long… upon Scotland".

pp. 274–284 · Recapitulation, self-commentary and petition
  • The axioms are run back through to "prove" each demand: liberty for himself, his brothers and servants; freedom from sequestration and garrisons; reward for his inventions.
  • A strange and very characteristic passage analyses the treatise's own rhetoric. The narrator says he chose an "Apodictick course by a compositive method", names the figures and tropes he has used, and explains why passion persuades better than "bare Logical arguments".
  • The formal petition asks Parliament to grant Urquhart his freedom from parole and his house of Cromarty free of its garrison. It also asks for his estate free of any debts not of his own making, the hereditary sheriffship, the patronage of three churches, and the admiralty of the seas between Caithness and Inverness. In return he offers "a benefit… of ten times more worth".
  • The book ends with the couplet that echoes its opening motto: "Pity it were to refuse such, As ask but little, and give much."
  • Two appendix lists follow: 8 Scottish generals and 119 colonels and officers named in the book. Then FINIS.
In some copies · The Cyfral Octastich

Some copies end with a page of 285 numbers, dated London, March 1651/2, and a verse explaining that the key is "gather'd out of my Exskybalorum". The cipher hides an eight-line royalist prayer for Charles II, "Great Lord, mantaine that regal familie…", ending "Amen, so be it". It is a private loyalty concealed in a book addressed to Cromwell's Parliament. It is missing from the copy filmed for Early English Books; see Cyfral_Octastich_Visualizer.html.

Purpose: what it was really for

Like Logopandecteision a year later, The Jewel is a petition in elaborate disguise. Each strand serves the plea:

  • The Universal Language is the collateral. It shows what Urquhart's mind can produce, and he says plainly it will be completed only "if he wanted not encouragement".
  • The vindication of Scotland removes the obstacle that, as he complains, "his being a Scot, and a prisoner of War" posed to his suit. If Scots in general are honourable and only the Presbyterians are vicious, a royalist Scot can be trusted.
  • The attack on the Kirk aligns him with his English captors, who were then dismantling Presbyterian power in Scotland, and turns his royalism into common cause against a shared enemy.
  • The case for union offers the Commonwealth exactly the policy it was pursuing, and casts Urquhart as one of its natural supporters.
  • Crichton and the catalogues entertain and dazzle. They are proof by example of what Scots, and by implication the descendant of 152 Urquhart chiefs, can achieve.

The hidden cipher, if genuine to these copies, shows the limits of that alignment. Beneath the courtesies to Parliament, Urquhart still prayed for the king.

How it is written

  • A fictional frame. The "found manuscript" and third-person narrator let Urquhart praise himself as if someone else were doing it.
  • Catalogues and superlatives. Lists of advantages, ancestors, generals, colonels and scholars overwhelm by sheer number.
  • Romance narrative. The Crichton episode borrows the conventions of chivalric romance: impossible feats, set-piece duels, a love scene, a tragic betrayal.
  • Coined and Latinate vocabulary ("Epassyterotically", "cartapaciatory", "homogeneated") shows off the linguistic invention he claims.
  • Borrowed authority. The long Bacon and Coke passages lend the union argument respectable English voices.
  • Self-conscious rhetoric. The book ends by analysing its own figures of speech, part display and part joke.
  • Speed as a boast. The fourteen-day composition is offered as proof that "extemporaneanness… may very probably be more successeful, then premeditation".
