Urquhart's Proquiritations

The closing section of Sir Thomas Urquhart's Logopandecteision (London, 1653), compared with the revised version in the Maitland Club reprint, The Works of Sir Thomas Urquhart (Edinburgh, 1834), pp. 412–416.

proquiritationnoun (Urquhart's coinage)

Most likely meaning: a public outcry or appeal for redress, a demand proclaimed openly before one's fellow citizens.

The word comes from the Latin verb quiritare, "to cry out for help". Literally it meant to call on the Quirites, the citizens of Rome, as a wronged person would in the street. The related noun quiritatio means a cry of distress or appeal for help. Latin dictionaries also record a rare compound, proquiritare, "to cry out publicly". The prefix pro- adds "forth, openly, before the people". A "proquiritation" is therefore a cry of grievance made in public, which fits exactly how Urquhart uses it: a numbered list of what he calls his "just demands" upon the State. The 1834 editors gloss the word the same way, describing the book as ending with "a fanciful summary of the author's just demands, or 'proquiritations' upon the State" (Introduction, p. xx). The word does not appear to have been used by anyone else.

What the section is and what it does

The Proquiritations are the book's closing argument. After six Books setting out his grievances against creditors, the Presbyterian Kirk and the courts, Urquhart compresses everything into 32 formal demands addressed to the governing "State", the English Commonwealth that held him as a prisoner on parole after the Battle of Worcester (1651).

  • Form. Each Proquiritation is a single long sentence in the style of a legal petition. It usually opens with a "Seeing [that]…" clause stating a fact, followed by "That…" stating what should be done. It closes with a formula such as "is the humble desire of", "is the hearty wish of" or "is the opinion of", followed by a pair of initials.
  • The initials. Each demand is "signed" by a different supporter: K. F., P. O., N. Wa., and so on. None has been identified, and they may be invented or anonymised. Their effect is to turn one man's petition into a chorus of backers, and the last item (Tn. Vs.) declares that all of it is the unanimous wish of everyone who has known him, "of any Nation".
  • The number 32. A short preface explains the choice. 32 was "by the Rabbies of old ascribed to Wisdom, and by Pythagoras to Justice". An "algebraical hexastick" (six-line verse) then shows that 32 is two to the fifth power: "Of Postulatas a sursolid" (a fifth power), whose "content" is twice "that square of squares" (16), "Which is the double of the cube of two". The verse ends with a pun: "How that square dealing will him best become".
  • Purpose. The section works as a summary for busy officials. It recaps the whole book as a checklist of concrete demands, each paired with a reason. Those reasons appeal to justice, to the State's self-interest and reputation, and to its ambitions for Scotland. Several items flatter the English regime, presenting Urquhart as a natural ally against the Presbyterian Kirk, which was the Commonwealth's enemy too.

The 1653 and 1834 versions

The 1834 reprint gives the same 32 Proquiritations under the same initials, but reordered and often rewritten. Only №18, 24, 25, 31 and 32 keep their place. The revision mostly removes direct appeals to "the State" and "the Conqueror" and references to Stirling, Worcester and the English conquest. It adds new details, such as release from being "detained as a prisoner of war". It also varies the closing formulas so that the final item can list them all. The 1834 text's order is the one the Cyphral Distich uses. No 1653 copy with the revised text has yet been identified; see the separate collation report for the evidence.
